Does banning prostitution move the money?

Twenty dated reforms, 1999–2022, against 214 countries of annual macro data. The United Kingdom has the richest data; South Korea has the only shock big enough to see. The answer is a bound, not a discovery — and the bound is tight enough to be worth having.

1

The question, and the chain it has to travel down

Banning or re-legalising prostitution is supposed to do something to the economy. The claim is usually about money moving faster or slower, not just about output. So the test has to follow a chain, and each link has a sign attached before any data is touched.

  • ↓OutputA ban suppresses part of a measured sector, or pushes it out of the accounts. Measured GDP falls by the suppressed share. A reinstatement does the reverse, and can raise GDP purely by making activity observable.
  • ↓VelocityV = PY/M. The central bank sets M, so a fall in measured Y is a fall in measured V of the same proportion. Bans should lower velocity; legalisation should raise it.
  • ↓MultiplierA banned trade settles in cash. Currency leaves deposits, the currency-to-deposit ratio rises, and m = (1+c)/(r+c) falls because ∂m/∂c < 0. Bans should lower the multiplier.

So the pre-registered prediction is a clean set of signs: ban → GDP down, velocity down, multiplier down; reinstatement → all three up. An estimate with the wrong sign, or one many times larger than the sector could mechanically supply, is evidence of confounding rather than of effect — and that turns out to be the whole story.

What the exercise actually establishes. 20 dated reforms across 18 jurisdictions, 1999–2022, 12 of them bans. The United Kingdom has the most abundant data by a clear margin. And the answer to the headline question is a bound rather than a discovery: outside South Korea the sector is one to three orders of magnitude too small to show up in annual GDP, velocity or multiplier data, and the currency-drain channel into the multiplier is undetectable even in Korea.

Of the 11 panel estimates below, 5 carry the wrong sign and 6 are more than five times the largest effect the trade could produce if it vanished entirely — the worst by a factor of 643. The two synthetic controls that reach p < 0.05 both collapse once the comparison group is tightened. Those are confounds — the dot-com cycle, the financial crisis, euro convergence — not prostitution policy.

The United Kingdom wins, at 0.911. It is the only jurisdiction strong on all four legs: 24 years of prostitution output inside official GDP (the ONS line, the largest official share anywhere at 0.37% of GDP), a national reform in 2010, a within-country reform in Northern Ireland in 2015 that leaves England, Wales and Scotland as controls, and 61 unbroken years of its own broad money.

The United States is a close second (0.904) and actually wins the measurement leg outright, with 53 years of BEA prostitution estimates — but they sit in a research satellite account rather than in GDP, the sector is measured at only 0.02% of GDP, and there has never been a national reform to test. South Korea ranks 4 on abundance yet matters more than its rank suggests: at 4.10% of GDP its sector is the only one anywhere large enough for the question to have any statistical power at all. So the rest of this report runs the panel on every testable event, and then looks hard at both the UK (most data) and Korea (biggest shock).

Euro-area members cluster at the bottom not because their prostitution data is poor — the Netherlands and Italy publish more of it than most — but because monetary union erased their national money stocks. Their velocity and multiplier legs are literally zero.

4

How big could the effect possibly be?

This has to come before any regression, because it sets the bar the regression then has to clear. No identification is involved — it is arithmetic on the size of the sector.

Δln Y = −φ·s   ·   V = PY / M  ⇒  Δln V ≈ Δln Y   ·   m = (1+c)/(r+c)  ⇒  ∂m/∂c = (r−1)/(r+c)2 < 0

A ban suppresses a fraction φ of a sector worth s per cent of GDP, so measured output falls by φ·s. Monetary policy, not the sex trade, sets the money stock M, so to a first order velocity falls by the same amount. The multiplier moves through a different door: the suppressed trade settles in cash, which raises the currency-to-deposit ratio c, and the multiplier falls in c. If a currency unit is re-spent n times a year, the extra currency held is φ·T/n.

5 of the 11 panel estimates carry the opposite sign to the mechanism and a further 2 have the right sign but a magnitude the sector cannot supply. A velocity estimate of +10.3% after a ban, when the trade in those countries averages 1.17% of GDP and so can account for at most that much even if it vanished outright, is not a finding about prostitution. It is what a seven-country treatment group looks like when the events cluster around 1999–2010 — euro convergence, the dot-com cycle, the financial crisis.

Four things that genuinely limit this

  • No national money for the euro area. Monetary union erased the national broad-money series, so the Netherlands 2000, Germany 2002 and 2017, France 2016 and Ireland 2017 cannot be tested on velocity or the multiplier at all. Canada drops out too — its broad-money series ends in 2008, six years before PCEPA.
  • No monetary base anywhere. The textbook m = M2/M0 is not constructible from any source reachable here, so the multiplier is approached through two ratio proxies and the analytic formula. The reserve ratio exists only from 2001, which leaves the 2004 Korean ban with three pre-years.
  • The reinstatement arm rests on three countries. Denmark 1999, Hungary 1999 and New Zealand 2003 are the only liberalisations with both GDP and money data. One of the credit-multiplier cells is identified off two countries and returns +80%, which is best read as a warning label rather than a result.
  • Annual data, and a measurement problem inside the outcome. The thing being banned is partly defined by whether statisticians count it. The UK and the Netherlands differ fivefold in their estimates of the same sector under the same ESA 2010 obligation, so part of any measured GDP movement around a reform is the accounting treatment changing, not behaviour.

What would actually settle it

Not more countries — more frequency and more locality. Korea is the only place where the shock is big enough to see, and the test it deserves is quarterly or monthly: Bank of Korea M1, M2 and base money, currency in circulation, and the 2004 break. Below that, the right design is sub-national and sector-specific: Rhode Island's 2003–2009 window against other US states on county business patterns, and Northern Ireland against Great Britain on regional GVA, both of which the ONS and BEA publish. Those sources are blocked by this environment's network policy; with them the same code would run on data that can actually carry the question.
