Mountains or beach?

1,000 campers each chose one of five Massachusetts state parks: two in the Berkshire mountains, three on the coast. The Department of Conservation and Recreation (DCR) wants to know how campers value travel time and setting, and what a $20 fee increase at Mount Greylock would do. The multinomial logit gives one answer; nested and mixed logits show why it is the wrong one.

The original problem set used mlogit() with nests and random parameters. Python has no such package, so the cells below write each likelihood in a few lines of numpy.

1a.i

Multinomial logit of park choice

Model the park choice as a multinomial logit with common parameters on cost, time and setting, \(V_{nj}=\beta_1C_{nj}+\beta_2T_{nj}+\beta_3M_j\), without alternative-specific intercepts. Report and interpret the estimates.

Theory

The setting enters through a park attribute \(M_j\), not an alternative-specific constant. That is the reason for the warning in the prompt: with constants for every park, \(\beta_3M_j\) would be a linear combination of the mountain parks' constants and could not be identified. Without constants, the model says that two parks with the same cost, time and setting are equally attractive on average.

\[P_{nj}=\frac{\exp(\beta_1C_{nj}+\beta_2T_{nj}+\beta_3M_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{5}\exp(\beta_1C_{nk}+\beta_2T_{nk}+\beta_3M_k)} .\]

The estimation is the conditional logit of Problem Sets 2 and 3, now on data that arrive already in long form. mlogit's formula visit ~ cost + time + mountain | 0 means three generic variables and no intercepts.

1a.ii

What do campers pay for time and setting?

Calculate the dollar value that a camper places on each hour spent traveling and on camping in the mountains (relative to the beach).

Theory

As in Problem Set 2, ratios of coefficients convert utility into dollars. The value of an hour of travel is \(60\,\beta_2/\beta_1\) (both negative, so the ratio is positive). The value of the mountain setting relative to the beach is the cost change that offsets it:

\[\beta_1\,\Delta C+\beta_3=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \Delta C=-\beta_3/\beta_1=\beta_3/(-\beta_1).\]

A negative value means the average camper would need to be paid to switch from an otherwise identical beach park to a mountain park.

1a.iii

Elasticities with respect to the Greylock fee

For each camper, calculate the elasticity of choosing each park with respect to the cost of camping at Mount Greylock (park 1). Report the mean elasticity for each park and relate the pattern to an important property of the logit.

Theory

For the logit, the elasticities with respect to the cost of park 1 are

\[E_{P_{n1},C_{n1}}=\beta_1C_{n1}(1-P_{n1}),\qquad E_{P_{nj},C_{n1}}=-\beta_1C_{n1}P_{n1}\quad(j\neq1).\]

The cross-elasticity is identical for all four other parks. A camper priced out of Greylock is no more likely, proportionally, to go to October Mountain than to the beach. That is IIA again, and here it is clearly implausible: someone who chose a mountain park probably likes mountains.

1b.i

The nested logit

If campers have individual preferences for mountains or beach, utilities are correlated among parks with the same setting. Model the choice as a nested logit with two nests (mountain: parks 1–2; beach: parks 3–5) and the same representative utility as in part (a). Report and interpret the estimates.

Theory

Suppose each camper has an unobserved taste for mountains or for the beach. Then the errors of the two mountain parks move together, and so do those of the three beaches. The nested logit allows exactly this. It belongs to the generalised extreme value (GEV) family: errors are still extreme value, but correlated within nests \(B_k\).

\[P_{ni}=\underbrace{\frac{e^{V_{ni}/\lambda_k}}{\sum_{j\in B_k}e^{V_{nj}/\lambda_k}}}_{P_{ni\mid B_k}}\ \times\ \underbrace{\frac{\big(\sum_{j\in B_k}e^{V_{nj}/\lambda_k}\big)^{\lambda_k}}{\sum_{l}\big(\sum_{j\in B_l}e^{V_{nj}/\lambda_l}\big)^{\lambda_l}}}_{P_{nB_k}} .\]

The term \(I_{nk}=\ln\sum_{j\in B_k}e^{V_{nj}/\lambda_k}\) is the inclusive value of nest \(k\), and \(\lambda_k\) is its coefficient (iv in mlogit). Interpretation:

  • \(\lambda_k=1\): no correlation within the nest; if all \(\lambda\)'s are 1, the model collapses to the multinomial logit.
  • \(0<\lambda_k<1\): positive correlation; \(1-\lambda_k\) is a rough index of it. Smaller \(\lambda\) means alternatives in the nest are closer substitutes.
  • \(\lambda_k>1\): inconsistent with utility maximisation for some data.

IIA now holds within a nest but not across nests.

The cost, time, and mountain parameters are interpreted as they were in part (a), although the mountain parameter is now statistically significant at the 5% level. This model contains two additional parameters, the coefficients on the inclusive value for each nest, which are statistically significant. These parameters represent the independence of random utility within each nest, with a value of 1 indicating full independence and a value close to 0 indicating high dependence. Thus, we conclude there is some dependence within each of the nests. That is, the random utility that a camper obtains from camping at each of the parks in the mountains is correlated, as is the random utility from camping at each of the parks at the beach. This result is consistent with each camper having an individual preference for the mountains or the beach beyond the average preference represented by β₃, the common mountain parameter.

1b.ii

Is the nesting needed?

The model in part (a) imposes a restriction on the model in part (b). Write the null hypothesis, describe it in words, and test it with a likelihood ratio test.

Theory

The MNL is the nested logit with \(\lambda_{mountain}=\lambda_{beach}=1\): two restrictions, so \(LR\sim\chi^2_2\) under \(H_0\). In words, the null is that the unobserved utility of each park is independent of every other park's, i.e. that campers have no individual preference for a setting beyond the average \(\beta_3\).

Illustrations Likelihood-ratio test
Solution key · course data, R output 
lrtest(model_1a, model_1b)
##   #Df  LogLik Df  Chisq Pr(>Chisq)
## 1   3 -1524.2
## 2   5 -1500.9  2 46.564  7.739e-11 ***

The null hypothesis is that the inclusive value coefficients equal 1 for both nests: \(H_0:\lambda_{mountain}=\lambda_{beach}=1\). In words, there is no correlation among the random utility terms, i.e. the random utility of every alternative is i.i.d. If a camper has an individual preference for camping in the mountains or at the beach beyond the average preference represented by β₃, this hypothesis would not hold. We reject it with a p-value of approximately 0: there is strong evidence of correlation among the random utility terms within each nest.

1b.iv

Elasticities from the nested logit

Calculate each park's elasticity with respect to the cost of camping at Mount Greylock for each camper, and report the mean for each park. Compare with part (a).

Theory

For park 1 in nest \(k\) (the mountains), differentiating the nested logit probability gives

\[E_{1,C_1}=\beta_1C_{1}\Big[\tfrac{1}{\lambda_k}-\tfrac{1-\lambda_k}{\lambda_k}P_{1\mid B_k}-P_{1}\Big],\] \[E_{j,C_1}=-\beta_1C_{1}P_{1}\Big[1+\tfrac{1-\lambda_k}{\lambda_k}\tfrac{1}{P_{B_k}}\Big]\ \ (j\in B_k,\ j\neq1),\qquad E_{j,C_1}=-\beta_1C_1P_1\ \ (j\notin B_k).\]

The extra term for parks in the same nest grows as \(\lambda_k\) falls: strong within-nest correlation means close substitutes. Across nests, the logit formula still applies.

2a.i

Mixed logit with random coefficients

Model the park choice as a mixed logit with \(V_{nj}=\beta_{1n}C_{nj}+\beta_{2n}T_{nj}+\beta_{3n}M_j\) and all three coefficients normal, \(\beta_k\sim\mathcal N(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\). Use 100 draws and seed 703. Report and interpret the estimates.

Theory

The nested logit gave each nest correlated errors. The mixed logit goes further: each camper has their own coefficients,

\[U_{nj}=\beta_n'x_{nj}+\varepsilon_{nj},\qquad \beta_{n}\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\ \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2)),\qquad \varepsilon\ \text{i.i.d. Gumbel}.\]

A camper with a large \(\beta_{3n}\) likes every mountain park. That taste creates correlation between the mountain parks' utilities, without our having to specify nests. Given \(\beta_n\) the choice is a logit, so the choice probability is an average of logits over the taste distribution:

\[P_{ni}=\int\frac{e^{\beta'x_{ni}}}{\sum_je^{\beta'x_{nj}}}\,\phi(\beta\mid\mu,\sigma)\,d\beta\ \approx\ \frac1R\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{e^{\beta_r'x_{ni}}}{\sum_je^{\beta_r'x_{nj}}},\quad \beta_r=\mu+\sigma\eta_r .\]

There is no closed form, so we simulate it with \(R=100\) standard normal draws \(\eta_r\) per camper (held fixed while the optimiser searches) and maximise the simulated log-likelihood. Problem Set 5 builds this estimator step by step. The sign of \(\sigma\) is not identified, since \(\sigma\eta\) and \(-\sigma\eta\) have the same distribution; only \(|\sigma|\) matters, which is why the key can report \(-5.6\).

2b.i

Mixed logit with a fixed cost coefficient

Model \(\beta_1\) as fixed and \(\beta_2,\beta_3\) as normal. Use 100 draws and seed 703. Report and interpret the estimates.

Theory

With a fixed cost coefficient, every dollar value is a random coefficient divided by a constant, so it keeps the coefficient's normal distribution. With a random cost coefficient, the value of time would be a ratio of two normals, which has no mean (the denominator can be arbitrarily close to zero). Fixing cost is the standard way to get well-behaved willingness-to-pay distributions.

2b.ii

Is the cost coefficient random?

Test whether β₁ is fixed or random. Write the null hypothesis, describe it in words, and conduct a likelihood ratio test.

Theory

\(H_0:\sigma_1^2=0\): every camper has the same marginal utility of money. One restriction, so compare \(LR\) with \(\chi^2_1\). Two caveats worth raising in class:

  • Boundary. A variance cannot be negative, so \(H_0\) sits on the edge of the parameter space. The correct null distribution is a 50:50 mixture of \(\chi^2_0\) and \(\chi^2_1\), so the usual p-value is too large by a factor of two (conservative).
  • Simulation. Both likelihoods are simulated. With the same draws in both models, simulation noise partly cancels, but the restricted model can even reach a higher simulated likelihood, as in the key (−1503.2 vs −1504.5). That is why the key prints a negative degrees-of-freedom difference.

## 1   6 -1504.5

The null hypothesis is that the variance of the random β₁ coefficient equals 0, \(H_0:\sigma_1^2=0\). In words, there is no heterogeneity in this cost coefficient, so all campers have the same marginal utility of cost or income. We fail to reject this hypothesis with a p-value of 0.11, so we conclude that the cost coefficient, β₁, is fixed for all campers.

2b.iii

Distributions of dollar values

Calculate the dollar value of an hour of travel and of camping in the mountains. Because β₂ and β₃ are random, these values are distributions: report the mean and standard deviation of each.

Theory

With a fixed cost coefficient, a random coefficient divided by \(-\beta_1\) is still normal:

\[\text{WTP}_{time,n}=60\,\frac{\beta_{2n}}{\beta_1}\sim\mathcal N\Big(60\frac{\mu_2}{\beta_1},\ \Big(60\frac{\sigma_2}{\beta_1}\Big)^2\Big),\qquad \text{WTP}_{mtn,n}=\frac{\beta_{3n}}{-\beta_1}\sim\mathcal N\Big(\frac{\mu_3}{-\beta_1},\ \Big(\frac{\sigma_3}{\beta_1}\Big)^2\Big).\]

A normal distribution of time values puts some mass on negative values, meaning campers who enjoy driving to the park. Whether that is plausible is a modelling choice; a log-normal coefficient would rule it out.

2c.i

Counterfactual: a $20 fee at Mount Greylock

DCR is considering a fee increase at Mount Greylock that would raise each camper's cost by $20. Using the model from part (b), how many fewer campers do you expect at Greylock, and how many more at each of the other four parks?

Theory

Predicted visits to park \(j\) are \(\sum_nP_{nj}\), where each \(P_{nj}\) is now the simulated mixed logit probability, the average over draws. Each camper's own draws make the substitution pattern realistic: draws with a high mountain taste put most weight on October Mountain when Greylock's price rises.

2c.ii

Who pays for the fee?

How do you expect the increased camping fee at Mount Greylock to affect the economic surplus of the 1000 campers?

Theory

With a fixed cost coefficient, the log-sum formula of Problem Set 2 applies draw by draw. For camper \(n\),

\[\Delta CS_n=\frac{1}{-\beta_1}\cdot\frac1R\sum_r\Big[\ln\sum_je^{V^{1}_{nrj}}-\ln\sum_je^{V^{0}_{nrj}}\Big].\]

The loss is close to $20 for a camper who would certainly have gone to Greylock and is near zero for one who would never go. Summed over campers, it is a little less than $20 times Greylock's visitors, because some of them switch parks.

This increased camping fee is expected to reduce the economic surplus of these 1000 campers by a total of roughly $4303.
