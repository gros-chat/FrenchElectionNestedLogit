1a

Means and medians of cost and time

The three factors most likely to drive the choice are the cost of driving (cost.car), the time to drive (time.car) and the time by bus (time.bus); the bus is free. (i) Report the mean and median of each for the full sample. (ii) Do you see patterns worth keeping in mind for the analysis?

Theory

The mean \(\bar x = \frac1N\sum_n x_n\) uses every observation, so a few extreme values pull it around. The median is the value with half the sample on each side; it ignores how far the extremes are. Comparing the two is a quick test of shape:

  • mean \(\approx\) median: roughly symmetric distribution;
  • mean \(>\) median: right skew, a long tail of large values;
  • mean \(<\) median: left skew.

Commute variables are almost always right-skewed. Distance from campus cannot fall below one mile here, but a few students live very far away, and time and fuel cost scale with distance. The skew matters for modelling: a linear model fitted on such data is driven partly by the tail, and a log transform or robust check may be worth considering.

The comparison of levels also matters. A choice model is about trade-offs. If driving is faster but costs money, the choice reveals how much students value their time. That idea, the value of travel time, returns in Problem 2.

1b

Do drivers and bus riders face different choices?

Students who drive and students who take the bus may face different times and costs. (i) Compute the same statistics separately for each group. (ii) Do you see patterns that begin to explain why some students drive and others take the bus?

Theory

Grouping by the outcome compares conditional distributions: the distribution of \(x\) given that the student drove, \(F(x\mid Y=1)\), against the distribution given that they took the bus, \(F(x\mid Y=0)\). If driving cost matters, drivers should on average face lower driving costs. This is the logic of Bayes' rule run backwards: a higher \(\Pr(Y=1\mid x)\) at low \(x\) shows up as more drivers with low \(x\).

These comparisons are one variable at a time, which is their weakness. The three variables are correlated because all of them grow with distance from campus. A student far from campus has a long drive and a long bus ride. So when we compare bus times across groups, we also compare driving times, and the two effects mix.

2a.i

The linear probability model

Model the choice to drive as a linear probability model, \[Y_n=\beta_0+\beta_1C_{nc}+\beta_2T_{nc}+\beta_3T_{nb}+\varepsilon_n,\] where \(Y_n=1\) if student \(n\) drives, \(C_{nc}\) is the cost of driving, \(T_{nc}\) the driving time and \(T_{nb}\) the bus time. Report the coefficients with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors and interpret them.

Theory

Why a regression on a 0/1 outcome is a probability model. Because \(Y_n\) only takes the values 0 and 1,

\[\mathbb E[Y_n\mid X_n] = 1\cdot\Pr(Y_n=1\mid X_n)+0\cdot\Pr(Y_n=0\mid X_n)=\Pr(Y_n=1\mid X_n).\]

OLS estimates a conditional mean, so here it estimates the probability of driving as a linear function \(X_n'\beta\). Each coefficient is a marginal effect in probability units: \(\partial\Pr(Y=1\mid X)/\partial x_k=\beta_k\), the same for every student.

Link to random utility. In the discrete-choice view, student \(n\) drives when \(U_{nc}>U_{nb}\). With utility linear in cost and time, the probability of driving is some increasing function of the utility gap \(V_n = \alpha + \gamma_1C_{nc}+\gamma_2T_{nc}+\gamma_3T_{nb}\). The LPM approximates that function with a straight line. Logit and probit (next problem sets) use an S-shaped curve instead. For students in the middle of the distribution the approximation is usually good; at the extremes it breaks (Question 2a.ii).

Why robust standard errors. The error \(\varepsilon_n=Y_n-p_n\) can take only two values, \(1-p_n\) or \(-p_n\), so

\[\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_n\mid X_n)=p_n(1-p_n),\qquad p_n = X_n'\beta.\]

The variance changes with \(X_n\): the LPM is heteroskedastic by construction. OLS is still unbiased, but the textbook formula \(\hat\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\) is wrong. The White (sandwich) estimator fixes it:

\[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta)=(X'X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_n \omega_n\,\hat\varepsilon_n^2\,x_nx_n'\Big)(X'X)^{-1}.\]

HC0 uses \(\omega_n=1\). R's vcovHC() defaults to HC3, \(\omega_n=1/(1-h_{nn})^2\), where \(h_{nn}\) is the leverage of observation \(n\). HC3 inflates the weight of high-leverage points and behaves better in small samples. We use HC3 in Python to match the key.

2a.ii

Fitted probabilities outside [0, 1]

One problem with a linear probability model is that predicted probabilities can fall outside the [0, 1] range. How many students have infeasible choice probabilities? Given these results, are you worried about using an LPM here?

Theory

Nothing in OLS forces \(\hat p_n = X_n'\hat\beta\) to lie in [0, 1]. A straight line with a non-zero slope must leave the unit interval somewhere; the question is whether many students sit out there.

Why it matters:

  • Interpretation. A fitted probability of 1.08 or −0.05 is meaningless, and predictions for policy scenarios (a bigger parking fee, say) can go out of range quickly.
  • Consistency. The LPM is consistent for the true marginal effects only if the true probability really is linear in \(X\). Many fitted values outside [0, 1] are evidence that the true curve bends, so the linear fit is misspecified and the slope is an average of a curve's slopes. Horrace and Oaxaca (2006) show the bias grows with the share of out-of-range predictions.
  • Variance. \(\hat p_n(1-\hat p_n)\) is negative out of range, so feasible GLS weights break down there.

A small share of out-of-range fits, as here, suggests that the linear approximation holds over most of the data. A calibration plot is a second check: group students by fitted probability and compare with the share who actually drive. If the LPM were right, the points would lie on the 45° line.

2a.iii

Are the two time coefficients equal in size?

Test whether the two time coefficients are equal in absolute value. Interpret the result and explain why it could make intuitive sense. If a delay raised the driving time and the bus time by the same amount, would the share of drivers rise, fall, or stay the same?

Theory

"Equal in absolute value" with opposite signs means \(\beta_2=-\beta_3\), a single linear restriction:

\[H_0:\ \beta_2+\beta_3=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R\beta=r,\qquad R=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\ r=0.\]

The Wald test asks whether the estimated \(R\hat\beta-r\) is far from zero relative to its sampling noise:

\[W=(R\hat\beta-r)'\big[R\,\widehat V\,R'\big]^{-1}(R\hat\beta-r)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \chi^2_q,\qquad F=W/q .\]

Here \(q=1\) restriction, so \(W\) is the square of a t-statistic: \(W=\big((\hat\beta_2+\hat\beta_3)/\operatorname{se}(\hat\beta_2+\hat\beta_3)\big)^2\), with \(\operatorname{se}^2=\widehat V_{22}+\widehat V_{33}+2\widehat V_{23}\). The covariance term matters: the two coefficients are estimated from correlated regressors. Using the robust \(\widehat V\) keeps the test valid under the LPM's heteroskedasticity. R's linearHypothesis() reports \(F=W/q\) compared with an \(F(q,\,N-K)\) distribution; for \(N=1000\) this is almost identical to \(\chi^2_1\), whose 5% critical value is 3.84.

Economics. If \(\beta_2+\beta_3=0\), only the difference in travel times matters: a minute is a minute in either mode. Rejecting it says a minute behind the wheel and a minute on the bus feel different. On the bus you can read or answer email; while driving in snow you cannot. An equal delay \(\Delta\) on both modes changes the probability of driving by \((\beta_2+\beta_3)\Delta\).

The Wald test indicates that the time coefficients are statistically different from one another. This is intuitive because driving and riding a bus are different experiences; for example, a student can read or catch up on email on the bus but not while driving. So a minute in each mode can affect the choice differently. The marginal effect of driving time is larger in absolute value, so an equal increase in the time of both modes would decrease the utility of driving and cause some drivers to switch to the bus.
