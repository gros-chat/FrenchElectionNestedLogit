Simulating tastes

Then we turn the question around and ask what a camper's choice reveals about their own tastes.

1a

Maximum simulated likelihood, by hand

Model the park choice as a mixed logit with a fixed cost coefficient and normal coefficients on time and mountain: \(V_{nj}=\beta_1C_{nj}+\beta_{2n}T_{nj}+\beta_{3n}M_j\), \(\beta_2\sim\mathcal N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\), \(\beta_3\sim\mathcal N(\mu_3,\sigma_3^2)\). Estimate by maximum simulated likelihood with 100 draws and seed 703. Report and interpret the estimates.

Theory

With a fixed cost coefficient and normal coefficients on time and setting, \(\beta_{2n}=\mu_2+\sigma_2\eta_{2n}\), \(\beta_{3n}=\mu_3+\sigma_3\eta_{3n}\), the probability that camper \(n\) chose park \(i\) is

\[P_{ni}(\theta)=\int L_{ni}(\beta)\,\phi(\eta)\,d\eta,\qquad L_{ni}(\beta)=\frac{e^{\beta'x_{ni}}}{\sum_j e^{\beta'x_{nj}}},\qquad\theta=(\beta_1,\mu_2,\mu_3,\sigma_2,\sigma_3).\]

Maximum simulated likelihood (MSL) replaces the integral by an average over \(R\) draws and maximises

\[\operatorname{SLL}(\theta)=\sum_n\ln\check P_{ni_n}(\theta),\qquad \check P_{ni}(\theta)=\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^{R}L_{ni}\big(\beta_1,\ \mu_2+\sigma_2\eta^{r}_{2n},\ \mu_3+\sigma_3\eta^{r}_{3n}\big).\]

Three practical points, all in the steps of the prompt:

  • Fix the draws. The standard normal \(\eta\)'s are drawn once (seed 703) and reused at every \(\theta\). New draws at each step would make the objective jump around and the optimiser would never converge.
  • Transform, don't redraw. Draws for \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) are \(\mu+\sigma\eta\), so changing \(\theta\) shifts and stretches the same draws smoothly.
  • Start close. The simulated likelihood is not globally concave and each evaluation is costly, so we start from Problem Set 4's estimates.

Steps I–III, written for one camper exactly as in the R solution:

One evaluation takes a noticeable fraction of a second, and BFGS needs many. The next cell computes the same number for all campers at once with 3-D arrays (campers × draws × parks) and checks that it matches. It also returns the exact gradient of the simulator, which BFGS uses instead of finite differences:

\[\frac{\partial\ln\check P_{ni}}{\partial\theta}=\sum_r w_{nr}\,\big(x_{ni}-\textstyle\sum_jL_{nj}(\beta^r)x_{nj}\big)'\,\frac{\partial\beta^r}{\partial\theta},\qquad w_{nr}=\frac{L_{ni}(\beta^r)}{R\,\check P_{ni}} .\]

Two practical fixes make the search reliable: the parameters are rescaled to similar sizes (cost is in thousandths, the mountain sd in units), and the search is run from both signs of \(\sigma_3\). With fixed draws the simulated likelihood is not exactly symmetric in the sign of \(\sigma\), so it can have one local maximum for each; we keep the higher one.

1b

Simulated elasticities

Use the model and draws from part (a) to simulate, for each camper, the elasticity of choosing each park with respect to the cost of camping at Mount Greylock (park 1). Report the mean elasticity for each park and interpret.

Theory

Differentiate the mixed logit probability with respect to the cost of park 1. Inside the integral each draw is a logit, so

\[E_{n1,C_{n1}}=\frac{C_{n1}}{P_{n1}}\int\beta_1L_{n1}(\beta)\big(1-L_{n1}(\beta)\big)\phi\,d\beta,\qquad E_{nj,C_{n1}}=-\frac{C_{n1}}{P_{nj}}\int\beta_1L_{n1}(\beta)L_{nj}(\beta)\,\phi\,d\beta .\]

The cross-elasticity now depends on \(j\) through the covariance of \(L_{n1}\) and \(L_{nj}\) across draws. In draws where the camper loves mountains, both Greylock and October Mountain have high probabilities, so October Mountain gains most. Simulate each integral with the same draws (steps vi–viii of the prompt).

2a.i

What does a choice reveal about tastes?

Use the model in problem 1(a) and the same draws to simulate each camper's conditional mean coefficients, weighting draws by the probability of the chosen park. Then average them over the campers at each park, and report the 3 × 5 = 15 mean coefficients.

Theory

The mixed logit estimates the population distribution \(g(\beta\mid\theta)\). Once we see a camper's choice \(y_n\), we can update it by Bayes' rule:

\[h(\beta\mid y_n,x_n,\theta)=\frac{P(y_n\mid x_n,\beta)\,g(\beta\mid\theta)}{P(y_n\mid x_n,\theta)} .\]

The conditional mean has a simple simulator using the same draws as estimation: weight each draw by how well it explains the observed choice,

\[\check{\bar\beta}_n=\sum_r w_{nr}\,\beta^{r},\qquad w_{nr}=\frac{L_{ny_n}(\beta^{r})}{\sum_{s}L_{ny_n}(\beta^{s})}.\]

These are not estimates of each camper's true coefficient (one choice is little information), but their averages over groups are informative. Averaging over the visitors of each park shows how tastes sort across parks.

2a.ii

Dollar values by park

Calculate the mean dollar value that campers at each park place on an hour of travel and on camping in the mountains (2 × 5 = 10 values). Interpret.

Theory

Convert the conditional means to dollars as before: \(60\,\bar\beta_{2}/\beta_1\) per hour of travel and \(\bar\beta_{3}/(-\beta_1)\) for the mountains. Because \(\beta_1\) is fixed, dividing the averaged coefficient is the same as averaging the individual dollar values.

The mean dollar value that a camper places on each hour spent traveling is approximately equal at all five parks. The mean dollar value of camping in the mountains (relative to the beach) differs substantially between the mountain parks and the beach parks. Campers who choose to camp in the mountains tend to place a positive value on camping in the mountains; campers who choose the beach tend to place a negative value on it, i.e. a positive value on the beach. In other words, the campers who choose a particular state park prefer the attributes of that park, which is intuitive.
