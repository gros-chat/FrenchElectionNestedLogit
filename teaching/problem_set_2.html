1a.i

The binary logit model

Model the choice to drive as a binary logit, \[\ln\Big(\frac{P_n}{1-P_n}\Big)=\beta_0+\beta_1C_{nc}+\beta_2T_{nc}+\beta_3T_{nb},\] where \(P_n\) is the probability that student \(n\) drives. Report the coefficients and standard errors and interpret them.

Theory

Random utility. Student \(n\) gets utility \(U_{nj}=V_{nj}+\varepsilon_{nj}\) from mode \(j\), where \(V_{nj}\) depends on what we observe (cost, time) and \(\varepsilon_{nj}\) on what we don't. The student drives when \(U_{nc}>U_{nb}\):

\[P_n=\Pr(\varepsilon_{nb}-\varepsilon_{nc}<V_{nc}-V_{nb}).\]

If each \(\varepsilon\) is i.i.d. type-I extreme value (Gumbel), their difference is logistic, and

\[P_n=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(V_{nc}-V_{nb})}}=\Lambda(x_n'\beta)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{P_n}{1-P_n}=x_n'\beta .\]

So the regression in the prompt is a model for the log-odds of driving. Each coefficient is a marginal utility: the change in the utility gap from one more dollar or minute. Its units are utils, and the util scale is set by fixing the variance of \(\varepsilon\) at \(\pi^2/3\). That is why coefficients are only meaningful relative to one another (2b.ii).

Estimation. There is no error term to minimise squares of, so we use maximum likelihood:

\[\ln L(\beta)=\sum_n \big[y_n\ln\Lambda(x_n'\beta)+(1-y_n)\ln(1-\Lambda(x_n'\beta))\big].\]

R's glm(family = "binomial") maximises it by Fisher scoring; statsmodels' Logit uses Newton's method. Both give the same answer. In Problem Set 3 you will write this likelihood yourself.

1a.ii

Marginal effects, student by student

Calculate the marginal effect of each independent variable for each student (3 variables × 1000 students = 3000 marginal effects). For each variable, report the mean, minimum, maximum and quartiles. Compare them with your LPM estimates from Problem Set 1.

Theory

In the logit, a coefficient is not a change in probability. Differentiating \(P_n=\Lambda(x_n'\beta)\):

\[\frac{\partial P_n}{\partial x_{nk}}=\beta_k\,P_n(1-P_n).\]

The marginal effect has the sign of \(\beta_k\), but its size varies by student. It peaks at \(\beta_k/4\) when \(P_n=1/2\) and shrinks toward 0 as \(P_n\) approaches 0 or 1. With 1,000 students and 3 variables there are 3,000 marginal effects; we summarise their distribution.

The average marginal effect (the mean of these) is the logit's counterpart of an LPM coefficient. The two are usually close, because OLS fits the average slope of the probability curve. What the logit adds is the heterogeneity around that average.

1a.iii

What is an hour worth?

Use your coefficient estimates to calculate the dollar value that a student places on each hour spent driving and on each hour spent on the bus.

Theory

Utility has no units, but a ratio of marginal utilities does. Hold utility fixed and trade time for money:

\[dV=\beta_{T}\,dT+\beta_C\,dC=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dC}{dT}\Big|_{V}=-\frac{\beta_T}{\beta_C}.\]

This is the value of time: the dollars that compensate one more minute of commuting, measured in $ per minute; multiply by 60 for $ per hour. The arbitrary util scale (and the variance of \(\varepsilon\)) cancels in the ratio.

The bus-time coefficient sits in the car utility with a positive sign: a longer bus ride makes driving relatively better. Its value of time uses \(|\beta_{T_b}/\beta_C|\), the money a student would give up for one less minute on the bus.

1b.i

Let the cost coefficient fall with income

Again model the choice as a binary logit, but let the parameter on cost vary inversely with income: \[\ln\Big(\frac{P_n}{1-P_n}\Big)=\beta_0+\frac{\beta_1}{I_n}C_{nc}+\beta_2T_{nc}+\beta_3T_{nb}.\] Report and interpret the coefficients.

Theory

The cost coefficient is the marginal utility of a dollar spent, i.e. minus the marginal utility of income. With the standard specification it is the same for every student. Dividing cost by income lets it fall as income rises:

\[V_{nc}-V_{nb}=\beta_0+\frac{\beta_1}{I_n}C_{nc}+\beta_2T_{nc}+\beta_3T_{nb},\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial C_{nc}}=\frac{\beta_1}{I_n}.\]

This is a common way to put observed heterogeneity into a logit: interact a coefficient with a characteristic of the decision maker. It follows from a utility that is linear in \(\ln(\text{income}-\text{cost})\), approximated for small costs. The new \(\beta_1\) is in utils per (dollar / $ thousand of income), so it is much larger in size than before; the values that matter are \(\beta_1/I\) at chosen incomes.

The value of time now varies with income too: \(\big|\beta_T/(\beta_1/I_n)\big|\times 60=|\beta_T/\beta_1|\times 60\, I_n\). Richer students value time more, as economic theory predicts.

2a.i

The multinomial logit

Model the spring commute choice as a multinomial logit with alternative-specific intercepts, a common cost parameter, and alternative-specific time parameters: \[V_{nj}=\alpha_j+\beta_1C_{nj}+\beta_jT_{nj}.\] Report and interpret the estimates.

Theory

With \(J\) alternatives and i.i.d. Gumbel errors, the probability that student \(n\) picks \(i\) is

\[P_{ni}=\frac{e^{V_{ni}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J}e^{V_{nj}}},\qquad V_{nj}=\alpha_j+\beta_1C_{nj}+\beta_jT_{nj}.\]

Only differences in utility matter. Adding a constant to every \(V_{nj}\) leaves all \(P_{ni}\) unchanged. So:

  • one alternative-specific constant must be fixed. mlogit sets the first alternative alphabetically, bike, to 0, so each \(\alpha_j\) is the average utility of mode \(j\) relative to biking, for equal cost and time;
  • a variable that is the same for every alternative (like income) can only enter with alternative-specific coefficients.

Generic vs. alternative-specific coefficients. Cost has one coefficient: a dollar is a dollar. Time has four, one per mode, because a minute biking and a minute on the bus may feel different. In mlogit's formula mode ~ cost | 1 | time, the three parts are generic variables, individual-specific variables (here just the constants), and alternative-specific coefficients.

Long data. The likelihood needs \(V_{nj}\) for every student and mode, so the data become an \(N\times J\times K\) array: one row per student-mode pair. That is what dfidx() builds in R and what long_design() builds below.

2a.ii

Elasticities and IIA

Calculate the elasticity of each alternative with respect to the cost of driving for each student (4 × 1000 = 4000 elasticities). Report the mean, minimum, maximum and quartiles for each alternative. How do these substitution patterns relate to an important property of the logit model?

Theory

The elasticity of \(P_{ni}\) with respect to the cost of driving \(C_{nc}\) follows from differentiating the logit formula:

\[E_{P_{nc},C_{nc}}=\beta_1 C_{nc}(1-P_{nc})\ \ \text{(own)},\qquad E_{P_{ni},C_{nc}}=-\beta_1 C_{nc}P_{nc}\ \ \text{for every } i\neq c\ \ \text{(cross)}.\]

The cross-elasticity does not depend on \(i\): biking, the bus and walking all gain the same percentage. This is the independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA). The ratio of any two probabilities,

\[\frac{P_{ni}}{P_{nk}}=e^{V_{ni}-V_{nk}},\]

depends only on \(i\) and \(k\). A change to the car cannot change how students split between bus and bike. If former drivers were more likely to switch to the bus (another motorised, weatherproof mode) than to walk, the logit would get the substitution wrong. That is the famous red bus / blue bus problem, and the reason for nested and mixed logits (Problem Sets 4 and 5).

2b.i

Single and married students, separately

Estimate the model from part (a) on two subsets: single students only, and married students only. Report and interpret the estimates.

Theory

Splitting the sample lets every coefficient differ between single and married students: different tastes for each mode, different sensitivity to cost and time. This is the most flexible way to model observed heterogeneity by a discrete characteristic. It costs precision, especially for the smaller group.

The pooled model of 2a is the special case where all coefficients are equal across groups. Because the pooled model is nested in the pair of split models, a likelihood ratio test compares them:

\[LR=2\big[(\ln L_{\text{single}}+\ln L_{\text{married}})-\ln L_{\text{pooled}}\big]\sim\chi^2_{K}.\]

A caveat: if only the scale of utility differs between groups (2b.ii), this test can reject even when tastes are identical.

2b.ii

Can the two cost coefficients be compared?

Can you use your estimates to compare the marginal utility of income for single students to that for married students? If so, describe the difference. If not, explain why not. (Hint: think about what component of the random utility model is assumed to be the same in both models.)

Theory

Write utility with its true scale: \(U_{nj}=V^*_{nj}+\sigma\varepsilon_{nj}\), with \(\varepsilon\) standard Gumbel. Dividing by \(\sigma\) does not change any choice, so the logit estimates

\[\beta=\frac{\beta^*}{\sigma}.\]

We never see \(\beta^*\) and \(\sigma\) separately. Each logit fixes \(\sigma\) at the same value by assumption. If married students' unobserved utility is less noisy (smaller \(\sigma\)), all their coefficients look larger, even with identical tastes \(\beta^*\). So comparing a cost coefficient of \(-2.71\) to one of \(-2.73\) across models does not compare marginal utilities of income.

Ratios are safe: \(\beta_k/\beta_l=\beta^*_k/\beta^*_l\). The scale cancels, which is why 2b.iii can compare values of time.

The marginal utility parameters are not directly comparable because they are estimated in different models. In every logit model, the variance of the random utility component has the same assumed value. In reality, however, this variance may differ for single and married students. As a result, the parameters of these models are estimated relative to the true variance of the random utility component; greater variance of random utility yields lower parameter estimates. Thus, if single students and married students have different variances of random utility, these models could yield different parameter estimates, even for the same underlying preferences and valuations of commute time. Conversely, these models could yield similar parameter estimates, even for very different underlying preferences and valuations of commute time, so long as the variances differ similarly.

2c.i

Counterfactual: buses 20% faster

More buses on existing routes would cut bus commute time by 20%. Using your estimates from part (a), how many additional students (of 1000) do you expect to take the bus? How many fewer will choose each of the other three modes?

Theory

A counterfactual changes the data, not the model. With estimates \(\hat\theta\), predicted demand for mode \(j\) is the sum of choice probabilities:

\[\widehat{D}_j=\sum_{n=1}^{N}P_{nj}(\hat\theta,\,x_n).\]

Recompute with bus time multiplied by 0.8 and take the difference. Under IIA the new bus riders are drawn from each mode in proportion to that mode's probability, student by student. Summing over students with different probabilities is what makes the aggregate pattern less rigid.

2c.ii

What is the policy worth?

How much additional economic surplus do you expect the reduction in bus commute time to generate for the 1000 students?

Theory

Students care about the best option they have. With Gumbel errors, the expected maximum utility has a closed form, the log-sum:

\[\mathbb E\big[\max_j U_{nj}\big]=\ln\Big(\sum_j e^{V_{nj}}\Big)+C .\]

The unknown constant \(C\) cancels in a change. Dividing by the marginal utility of income \(\alpha_n=-\beta_C\) converts utils to dollars:

\[\Delta CS_n=\frac{1}{\alpha_n}\Big[\ln\sum_j e^{V^{1}_{nj}}-\ln\sum_j e^{V^{0}_{nj}}\Big].\]

This requires utility to be linear in income (no income effects), which holds here because cost enters linearly. Summing over the 1,000 students gives the daily surplus gain to compare with the cost of running more buses.
